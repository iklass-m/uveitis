How to use it

Underlined terms open a definition. The result panel updates as you answer, and each recommendation lists the finding that triggered it. The rules follow common UK practice (NICE CKS, NICE NG128, BSR GCA guideline and standard neuro-ophthalmology teaching). They do not replace local protocols, senior advice or radiology discussion.

1Which eye? The cover test

Ask: "Next time it happens, cover one eye, then the other. Does it disappear with one eye covered?"

2What exactly do they see?

Ask: "Describe exactly what you see. Shape, colour, how long it lasts, whether it moves or spreads." Pick the closest match; the description of each opens when selected.

2Measure in light and dark

Use a pupil gauge or ruler with the patient fixating a distance target. Bright: room lights on plus a torch from below. Dark: lights off, dim side illumination, read at about 5 s. Phone photos are useful.

Right (mm)Left (mm) Bright light Dark

Rule used: anisocoria at least 0.5 mm greater in the dark means the small pupil is abnormal (it fails to dilate). At least 0.5 mm greater in light means the large pupil is abnormal (it fails to constrict). An RAPD never causes anisocoria.
